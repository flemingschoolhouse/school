Week 3 · Matter & Its Changes · States of Matter
Nevaeh

All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms and molecules) that are always moving. In a solid, particles are packed tightly and only vibrate in place, so a solid keeps its own shape and volume. In a liquid, particles are close but slide past each other, so a liquid has a set volume but takes the shape of its container. In a gas, particles are far apart and move fast in every direction, so a gas spreads out to fill any container. Adding thermal energy (heat) makes particles move faster — that's what causes phase changes: melting (solid → liquid), freezing (liquid → solid), evaporation/boiling (liquid → gas), condensation (gas → liquid), and sublimation (solid → gas, like dry ice). Water melts at 0 °C (32 °F) and boils at 100 °C (212 °F) at sea level.

Kitchen Science Lab · Dancing Raisins
Nevaeh

Materials: 2 clear glasses, clear soda (lemon-lime or club soda), plain water, 6–8 raisins per glass.

Why it works: soda is full of dissolved carbon dioxide gas. A raisin is denser than the soda, so it sinks. But its wrinkly surface gives the gas lots of spots to form bubbles. When enough bubbles stick, they act like tiny life jackets — the raisin's overall density drops below the soda's and it rises (buoyancy). At the top the bubbles pop into the air, the raisin gets denser again, and down it goes. It keeps “dancing” until the soda goes flat.

Week 3 · Matter & Its Changes · Physical vs. Chemical Changes
Nevaeh

A physical property can be observed without changing what the substance is: color, hardness, density, melting point, whether it's magnetic, whether it dissolves. A chemical property describes how a substance can change into a new substance: flammability (can it burn?), ability to rust, reacting with acid.
A physical change changes size, shape, or state, but it is still the same stuff (cutting, melting, dissolving). A chemical change makes a new substance with new properties (burning, rusting, cooking, digesting). Physical changes are often easy to undo; chemical changes usually are not.

DAY 2
History
Week 3 · The Road to Revolution · The Stamp Act, 1765
Nevaeh

To pay its war debt, Parliament passed the Sugar Act (1764) and then the Stamp Act (1765), the first tax placed directly on the colonists. Newspapers, legal documents, licenses, pamphlets, and even playing cards had to carry a paid tax stamp. Colonists were furious — not mostly about the money, but because they had no representatives in Parliament. Their cry: “No taxation without representation!” Groups called the Sons of Liberty protested, sometimes violently. Nine colonies sent delegates to the Stamp Act Congress, and merchants boycotted (refused to buy) British goods. Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in 1766 — but passed the Declaratory Act, saying it could make laws for the colonies “in all cases whatsoever.” In 1767 the Townshend Acts taxed glass, lead, paint, paper, and tea.

Week 3 · Matter & Its Changes · Chemical Reactions
Nevaeh

In a chemical reaction, atoms rearrange to make new substances. The starting substances are the reactants; the new ones are the products. A chemical equation shows them with an arrow that means “yields”:  reactants → products. Rust: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ (iron + oxygen → iron oxide).
Signs a reaction happened: ① gas bubbles (not from boiling), ② color change, ③ temperature change or light given off, ④ a precipitate (a solid forms when two liquids mix), ⑤ a new odor. Baking soda + vinegar fizzes because it makes carbon dioxide gas.

DAY 3
History
Week 3 · The Road to Revolution · Boston Massacre & Tea Party
Nevaeh

Britain sent soldiers to keep order in Boston, and tension grew. On March 5, 1770, a crowd taunted and threw snowballs at British soldiers, who fired into it and killed five colonists, including Crispus Attucks, a man of African and Native American descent. Patriots called it the Boston Massacre, and Paul Revere's famous engraving spread anger through the colonies. Yet lawyer John Adams defended the soldiers in court because he believed everyone deserved a fair trial; most were found not guilty. Parliament dropped the Townshend taxes — except the tax on tea. After the Tea Act (1773), on December 16, 1773, Sons of Liberty disguised as Mohawk Indians boarded three ships and dumped 342 chests of tea into Boston Harbor: the Boston Tea Party.

Apostrophes do two jobs.
① Contractions — the apostrophe replaces missing letters: do not → don't, it is → it's, they are → they're, will not → won't.

Week 3 · Matter & Its Changes · Conservation of Mass
Nevaeh

The law of conservation of mass: in a chemical reaction, matter is not created or destroyed — atoms are only rearranged. So the total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products (in a closed container). French chemist Antoine Lavoisier showed this in the late 1700s.
Counting atoms: a subscript (small number) counts the atoms of the element just before it: H₂O has 2 H and 1 O. A coefficient (big number in front) multiplies the whole molecule: 3H₂O has 6 H and 3 O. An equation is balanced when every element has the same number of atoms on both sides.

Week 3 · The Road to Revolution · 1774–1775: War Begins
Nevaeh

Parliament punished Boston with the Coercive Acts (1774) — colonists called them the Intolerable Acts. They closed Boston Harbor until the tea was paid for, cut back Massachusetts' self-government, and let troops be housed in colonists' buildings. Other colonies sent Boston help. In September 1774, delegates from 12 colonies (all but Georgia) met in Philadelphia as the First Continental Congress. They agreed to boycott British goods and petition King George III. On the night of April 18, 1775, Paul Revere and William Dawes rode to warn that British troops were marching to seize weapons at Concord. On April 19, 1775, militia (“minutemen”) faced the British at Lexington and Concord — the first battles of the American Revolution, the “shot heard round the world.”
