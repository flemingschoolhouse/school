Week 3 · Human Body Systems · Skeletal & Muscular Systems
Nylah

Last week you learned cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism. This week we tour your organ systems! The skeletal system is your bones — an adult has 206. Bones support your body, protect organs (the skull guards the brain, the ribs guard the heart and lungs), store minerals like calcium, and make blood cells in the marrow. Bones meet at joints. The muscular system moves you. Muscles can only pull, so they work in pairs: your biceps pulls to bend your arm; your triceps pulls to straighten it. Tendons attach muscles to bones. There are three muscle types: skeletal (you control it), smooth (stomach, blood vessels), and cardiac (heart only).

Week 3 · The Bill of Rights · Why We Have a Bill of Rights
Nylah

When the Constitution was written in 1787, many Americans were worried. The Anti-Federalists feared a strong national government could take away people's freedoms, the way the British king had. They wanted a written list of rights the government could never take away. To win their support, James Madison — often called the “Father of the Constitution” — wrote amendments and introduced them in the First Congress in 1789. Congress sent 12 amendments to the states, and 10 were approved (ratified) on December 15, 1791. Those first ten amendments are called the Bill of Rights. An amendment is a change or addition to the Constitution.

Kitchen Science Lab · Dancing Raisins
Nylah

Materials: 2 clear glasses, clear soda (lemon-lime or club soda), plain water, 6–8 raisins per glass.

Why it works: soda is full of dissolved carbon dioxide gas. A raisin is denser than the soda, so it sinks. But its wrinkly surface gives the gas lots of spots to form bubbles. When enough bubbles stick, they act like tiny life jackets — the raisin's overall density drops below the soda's and it rises (buoyancy). At the top the bubbles pop into the air, the raisin gets denser again, and down it goes. It keeps “dancing” until the soda goes flat.

Week 3 · Human Body Systems · Circulatory System
Nylah

The circulatory system is your body's delivery service. The heart, a muscle about the size of your fist, pumps blood through blood vessels. The heart has 4 chambers: two upper atria and two lower ventricles. Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart; tiny capillaries connect them and trade oxygen and nutrients for wastes. Blood has red blood cells (carry oxygen), white blood cells (fight germs), platelets (help clots form to stop bleeding), and liquid plasma. Your heart beats about 100,000 times a day!

Nylah

Sentences have four jobs, and each job has its own end mark. A declarative sentence tells something and ends with a period (We go to church on Sunday.). Week 3 · Human Body Systems · Respiratory System
Nylah

The respiratory system brings oxygen in and sends carbon dioxide out. Air enters your nose or mouth, travels down the trachea (windpipe), splits into two bronchi, and fills your two lungs. Inside the lungs are millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli, wrapped in capillaries. There, oxygen moves into the blood and carbon dioxide moves out — that's how the respiratory and circulatory systems work together. A dome-shaped muscle under your lungs, the diaphragm, pulls down to help you breathe in and relaxes up to help you breathe out.

Nylah

The digestive system breaks food into nutrients small enough to get into your blood. Digestion starts in the mouth: teeth chew and saliva begins breaking food down. The esophagus squeezes food down to the stomach, which churns it with acid. Next comes the small intestine — about 20 feet long! — where most nutrients are absorbed into the blood. Nylah

Amendments 5–8 protect people accused of crimes, so trials are fair. 5th: you can't be tried twice for the same crime (double jeopardy), you can't be forced to testify against yourself (“pleading the Fifth”), and you can't lose life, liberty, or property without due process (fair legal steps). 6th: a speedy and public trial by an impartial jury, the right to know the charges, to question witnesses, and to have a lawyer. 7th: a jury trial in many civil cases (disagreements over money or property, not crimes). 8th: no excessive bail or fines and no cruel and unusual punishment.
