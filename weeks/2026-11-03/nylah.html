Nylah

A noun names a person, place, thing, or idea (sister, Florida, guitar, courage). Common nouns are general (city); proper nouns name a specific one and are capitalized (Jacksonville). Science
Week 2 · Cells: Building Blocks of Life · Cell Theory
Nylah

In 1665, Robert Hooke looked at a thin slice of cork under an early microscope and saw tiny boxes. He called them cells. Over the next 200 years, scientists discovered that every living thing is built from cells. This became the cell theory: (1) all living things are made of one or more cells; (2) the cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things; (3) all cells come from other cells. Some living things are a single cell (unicellular, like bacteria); you are multicellular — about 30 trillion cells, all working together!

Week 2 · The Three Branches · The Constitution
Nylah

After winning independence, America first tried the Articles of Confederation, but the national government was too weak. In 1787, delegates met in Philadelphia and wrote the U.S. Constitution, the supreme law of the land. It begins with the Preamble — “We the People” — showing that power comes from the citizens. To keep any one group from getting too powerful, the Constitution splits power three ways: separation of powers. The Legislative branch makes the laws, the Executive branch carries out the laws, and the Judicial branch interprets the laws. The first 10 amendments, the Bill of Rights, protect freedoms like speech and religion.

Kitchen Science Lab · Magic Milk
Nylah

Materials: a shallow plate, milk (whole works best), food coloring, dish soap, cotton swabs.

Why it works: milk is mostly water with tiny bits of fat and protein floating in it. The surface of the milk is pulled tight like a stretched skin — that's surface tension. Soap weakens that pull right where it touches, so the tighter surface around it yanks outward, dragging the colors. Soap molecules also grab onto fat, and as they chase the fat around, the swirling keeps going.

Nylah

Adjectives describe nouns — they tell which one, what kind, how many (three small puppies). The articles a, an, the are adjectives too. Adverbs describe verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs — they tell how, when, where, how much (She sang beautifully. We left early. It was very cold.). Nylah

Plant cells have everything animal cells have, plus three extras. A stiff cell wall outside the membrane gives support (that's why celery is crunchy). In multicellular living things, cells are organized in levels, from smallest to largest: cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism. A tissue is a group of similar cells doing the same job (muscle tissue). An organ is made of different tissues working together (the heart). An organ system is a group of organs with one big job (the circulatory system: heart, blood vessels, blood). All the systems together make an organism — you!

Week 2 · The Three Branches · Judicial Branch + Checks & Balances
Nylah

Article III creates the judicial branch: the Supreme Court and lower federal courts. The Supreme Court has 9 justices who serve for life so they can decide cases fairly without worrying about re-election. Courts decide what laws mean, and through judicial review they can strike down a law that goes against the Constitution. Checks and balances let each branch limit the others: the President can veto a bill; Congress can override a veto with a 2/3 vote; the Senate approves judges; courts can rule laws unconstitutional.

Week 2 · Latitude & Longitude
Nylah

Latitude lines run east–west (like rungs on a ladder) and measure how far north or south of the equator a place is (0° to 90°). Longitude lines run north–south, pole to pole, and measure how far east or west of the prime meridian a place is (0° to 180°). Write latitude first: Jacksonville is about 30°N, 82°W.
